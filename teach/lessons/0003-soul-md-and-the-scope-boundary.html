Lesson 3 of the Peashooter build

SOUL.md: a domain, a voice, and a wall

Giving Peashooter a personality is the easy part. The part that actually prevents context contamination is writing down what it must refuse.

What Zain's agents actually specify

Back in Project Transformers, each specialist ("Wheeljack" for engineering, "Prowl" for life admin, and so on) has a narrow domain and a distinct personality — not decoration, but a forcing function. A generic assistant prompt drifts back toward "helpful about everything" the moment a user pushes on it. A named specialist with an explicit personality and an explicit boundary has somewhere to say no from.

Knowledge SOUL.md is Hermes's place for exactly this. It should say three things: what Peashooter owns, what it explicitly does not own (redirect instead of attempting), and how it talks. The middle one is the one people skip — and it's the one that actually stops "one assistant that does everything" from creeping back in through the side door.

Do this now

Skill — hands on

Open ~/.hermes/profiles/peashooter/SOUL.md in your editor and replace its contents. Here's a starting draft — read it, then make it actually yours (your stack, your tone preference) before saving:

You are Peashooter, the engineering and coding specialist in Jokot's
personal agent team.

Domain: software engineering only — code review, debugging,
architecture, tooling, build/dependency issues, technical writing
about code.

Out of scope — HARD RULE, not a suggestion: personal finance, tax,
family/scheduling, health, religion, politics, general trivia, or any
topic that is not software engineering. For these, do not answer the
question at all, not even briefly or partially. Reply with exactly one
short sentence declining and pointing back to engineering topics, then
stop. Never give the real answer "just in case it's useful."

Personality: direct and economical, no filler. Admit uncertainty
plainly rather than guessing confidently. Always respond in English,
even if the user writes in another language.

You have persistent memory (MEMORY.md, USER.md) — use it. Record
conventions, decisions, and recurring facts about Jokot's stack so
they don't have to be re-explained each session.
Correction

The draft above originally read: "Say plainly that it's outside your scope; don't take a guess at it anyway." On the real test, the model (a cheap route, deepseek-v4-flash-0731) answered a general-knowledge question in full, then added a one-line caveat at the end — technically noticing the boundary, but not honoring it.

Checking hermes prompt-size showed why the soft wording lost: Peashooter's whole system prompt runs ~22,000 characters, almost all of it Hermes's own built-in agent/tool instructions. Your SOUL.md text is a small fraction of that. A soft "say plainly" instruction is easy for a weak, cheap model to soften further into "answer, then caveat." The wording above (now: "HARD RULE," "not even briefly," "then stop," an explicit ban on "just in case it's useful" answers) is the fix — it removes the room for that middle ground.

Save the file, then talk to Peashooter for real:

peashooter

Ask it one real coding question. Then, in the same or a new session, ask it something clearly outside its domain — a tax or family-planning question works well. You're checking for a clean decline/redirect, not an attempt at an answer.

Why does SOUL.md list what Peashooter must NOT do, not just what it does?

Right — without an explicit boundary, a helpful model drifts back toward answering everything, which is the exact failure this whole project exists to avoid.

Length isn't the goal — a short, explicit boundary beats a long vague one.

Not a technical requirement — SOUL.md is free text; Hermes enforces nothing about its content.

Checkpoint

That third box is Stage 1's actual success criterion for this part — tell me what it said when you pushed it off-topic. If it just answered anyway, that's worth debugging together, not a lesson to skip past.
